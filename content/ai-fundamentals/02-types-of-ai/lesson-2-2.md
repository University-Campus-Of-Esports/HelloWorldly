## Rules and learning

A **rule-based system** applies explicit instructions, often written as `if–then` rules. For example, a simple troubleshooting tool might ask whether a device has power and then follow a fixed decision tree. Its behaviour is relatively easy to trace, but people must anticipate and encode the relevant situations. It does not automatically improve from examples.

**Machine learning** takes a different approach. An algorithm uses data to fit a model that captures useful patterns. Given labelled examples of wanted and unwanted email, a model might learn features that help it classify new messages. Developers still make important choices about data, objectives and evaluation; the computer is not learning without guidance.

**Deep learning** is a family of machine-learning techniques based on neural networks with many processing layers. It has proved effective for complex inputs such as images, speech and language. Deep learning is therefore inside machine learning, which is itself one approach within the wider field of AI.

**Generative AI** describes systems that create new content rather than only assigning a category or score. Generative models can produce text, images, sound or other data. Many current generative systems use deep learning, but the terms are not interchangeable. A deep-learning system that labels an X-ray is not necessarily generative, while generative modelling existed before today’s large neural networks.

Real products commonly combine approaches. A customer-service tool might use a language model to draft an answer, fixed rules to prevent certain actions and conventional software to retrieve an account record. The useful question is not “Which single kind of AI is this?” but “Which approaches are used for each part, and what are their strengths and limits?”
