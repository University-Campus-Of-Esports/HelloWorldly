## Specialised intelligence

**Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI)** is AI designed or trained for a limited set of tasks. A chess program can select excellent moves but cannot use that skill to diagnose an illness. A speech-recognition model can turn audio into words but does not automatically know how to drive a car. Nearly all deployed AI is best understood as narrow AI.

“Narrow” does not mean weak. A specialised system may outperform people within its particular domain because it can process many possibilities quickly or detect subtle statistical patterns. Its success does not imply human-like understanding outside that domain.

Some modern systems appear less narrow because they can answer questions, summarise text, write code and discuss many subjects. These behaviours can all arise from one broadly trained model. However, varied outputs are not by themselves proof that a system possesses the flexible understanding, dependable reasoning and ability to learn across contexts associated with human intelligence.

## The idea of general intelligence

**Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)** is a proposed kind of AI that could understand, learn and apply knowledge across a very wide range of intellectual tasks. It would adapt to unfamiliar problems rather than being useful only within a defined area. There is no single accepted technical test for AGI, and experts disagree about its exact definition and possible arrival.

AGI should therefore be treated as **hypothetical**, not as an established product category. A company may use the phrase differently from a researcher, and confident predictions about future capabilities are uncertain.

When evaluating a system, ask what evidence exists for the particular task you care about. A model that writes fluent answers may still fail at arithmetic, invent a reference or misunderstand a real-world situation. Describing tested capabilities is more informative than applying a broad label.
