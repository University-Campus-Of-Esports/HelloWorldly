## Sources of risk

AI systems learn from or are evaluated using data shaped by the real world. If a dataset under-represents a group, contains historical discrimination or uses an unsuitable label, the resulting system can reproduce or amplify unfair patterns. **Dataset bias** does not always mean someone deliberately inserted prejudice; it can also arise from sampling, measurement and design choices.

Reliability is another concern. A model can work well on average but fail in a particular setting. An image model trained on clear daytime photographs might be unreliable in fog. A generative model can produce an invented quotation or source, sometimes called a hallucination or fabrication. Confident wording is not a measure of truth.

AI also creates **privacy** questions. Training or operating data may include personal, confidential or copyrighted material. Removing a name may not be enough to protect someone if other details can be combined to identify them. Users should avoid entering sensitive information into a system unless its data handling is understood and authorised.

Good evaluation examines more than one overall accuracy figure. Developers can test relevant groups and conditions, document limitations, monitor performance after release and provide a way to report problems. The acceptable error rate depends on the consequence: a poor film recommendation is inconvenient, while an error affecting healthcare or employment can be severe.

**Human oversight** should be meaningful. A person needs enough information, time and authority to question or reject an output, rather than simply approving it automatically. Responsibility remains with the people and organisations choosing to build, deploy and use the system. AI can support a decision, but it cannot take moral or legal accountability away from those actors.
